# Tyndale, More, and the English vocabulary of the Bible

English series, installment: *The newe Testament*, Worms, 1526.

The anchor is the translated book as printed in the target language, as in the Euclid installment (*Jihe yuanben*, Beijing, 1607): the small octavo New Testament that Peter Schoeffer printed at Worms in 1526, identified as his from its types and illustrations. Its title page, which survives only in the Stuttgart copy, reads "The newe Testamēt as it was written/ and caused to be writtē/ by them which herde yt" and names neither translator nor printer. Three copies survive: the British Library's (C.188.a.17, formerly Bristol Baptist College, complete except for the title leaf), St Paul's Cathedral's (incomplete) and the Württembergische Landesbibliothek's in Stuttgart, the only copy with its title page. Its companion is the Pentateuch of 1530, five books printed in Antwerp under the false imprint "Malborow in the lande of Hesse/ by me Hans Luft". For most of the words this episode follows, it is the Oxford English Dictionary's earliest evidence. Thomas More is in the title because he attacked the words themselves, and because the 1611 translators settled one of his objections in his favour.

## 2. The post (single long-form post, no reply chain)

49.63° N, 8.36° E · Worms · 1526

In 1526 a printer at Worms finished a small New Testament in English. Its title page did not name the translator. He was William Tyndale, an English priest who had left England two years earlier, and the book was forbidden in England before the first copy arrived.

The rule it broke was more than a century old. A constitution adopted by a church council at Oxford and ratified in London in January 1409 ordered "that no one henceforth on his own authority translate any text of Holy Scripture into the English or other language", and that no such translation be read "until the translation itself shall have been approved by the diocesan of the place or if need be by a provincial council." In 1523 Tyndale went to London to seek a place in the service of Cuthbert Tunstall, Bishop of London. He brought the king's comptroller, Sir Harry Gilford, an oration of Isocrates that he had translated from Greek into English, and Gilford spoke to the bishop for him. In Tyndale's account, "my lorde answered me, his house was full". Tyndale wrote later that he "understode at the laste not only that there was no rowme in my lorde of londons palace to translate the new testament, but also that there was no place to do it in all englonde." He left England in 1524.

He began printing at Cologne in 1525. The fullest account comes from an opponent, the Catholic controversialist Johann Cochlaeus, writing in 1549. The printers had three thousand copies in the press and had reached the quire signed K when Cochlaeus told Hermann Rinck, a Cologne patrician, and Rinck had the city council stop the work. Tyndale and his assistant, William Roye, took the printed sheets up the Rhine to Worms. One copy of the Cologne sheets survives in the British Library. It has thirty-one leaves, with Tyndale's prologue and Matthew as far as chapter 22. At Worms, Peter Schoeffer printed the whole New Testament as a small octavo without the Cologne marginal notes. The book was identified as Schoeffer's work from its types and illustrations. Three copies survive, in the British Library, in St Paul's Cathedral and in the Württembergische Landesbibliothek in Stuttgart, where the library identified its copy in 1996. The Stuttgart copy has the only surviving title page.

Tyndale translated from the Greek New Testament that Erasmus had printed, with the Latin Vulgate, Erasmus's Latin and Luther's German as aids. In an epilogue he asked learned readers to excuse the roughness of the work, "howe that I had no man to counterfet/ nether was holpe with englysshe of eny that had interpreted the same". He called the book "a thing begunne rather then fynnesshed" and promised a revision.

Copies reached England in 1526. On 24 October the Bishop of London ordered every copy in his diocese delivered to his vicar-general within thirty days, and at the end of the month copies were burned at a sermon at St Paul's Cross. Cochlaeus adds, as something that was said, that Tunstall told a great sermon audience in London he had found more than two thousand corruptions in the book. In a letter dated 24 February, almost certainly of 1527, the bishop's chaplain Robert Ridley complained that "by this translation shal we losse al thies Christian wordes, penance, charite, confession, grace, prest, chirche". In 1529 Thomas More named three words "of gret weyght". Where the church said priest, Tyndale wrote senior, for church he wrote congregation, and for charity he wrote love. More gave them in his own spelling as "senyours", "the congregacyon" and "loue". The 1526 print spells them "seniours" or "senyours", "congregacion" and "love". Tyndale answered in 1531 that where he "found this word ecclesia" he "interpreted it by this word congregation".

By then he had learned Hebrew. The five books of Moses carry a colophon at the end of Genesis, dated 17 January 1530, that gives the place and printer as "Malborow in the lande of Hesse/ by me Hans Luft". Both names are false, and the books were printed in Antwerp. Genesis and Numbers are in a gothic type and the other three books in a roman type. Bibliographers name either one printer, Johannes Hoochstraten, or two, Merten de Keyser and Joannes Grapheus. In June 1530 a royal proclamation ordered every printed English New or Old Testament handed in to the bishop. In 1906 John Rothwell Slater compared the Pentateuch with the Hebrew, the Vulgate and Luther's German of 1523. He found a few renderings where Tyndale follows the Hebrew against every other version, and he concluded that Tyndale probably drafted from Luther and checked his draft against the Hebrew throughout. Slater called it the first version in English made from the Hebrew.

Most of the words in this story are first recorded in the Pentateuch. In Exodus 12 the Vulgate calls the feast "phase, id est transitus domini", and the later version of the Wycliffite Bible, made from the Latin in the late fourteenth century, says "fase, that is, the passyng of the Lord". His word is a noun formed from the English verb "pass over". Exodus 12:11 reads "it is the Lordes passeouer", and two verses later, on the same page, "I will passe ouer you". His margin explains the name: "The lambe was called passeouer that the very name itself shuld put them in remembraunce what it signified." In Leviticus 16, Aaron casts lots over two goats, "one lotte for the Lorde, and another for a scapegoote". The second goat is the one "on which the lotte fell to scape", and it is let "goo fre in to the wildernesse". The Vulgate calls it "capro emissario", the goat sent away, and Luther calls it "dem freybock". For the gold lid of the ark, the Vulgate's propitiatorium, Tyndale wrote "merciseate", after Luther's Gnadenstuhl. For the loaves set out before God, the Vulgate's "panes propositionis", he wrote "shewbred", after Luther's Schaubrot, and his margin gives the reason: "Shewbred because it was alway in the presence and sight of the Lorde." Where the Vulgate and Luther give God's name in Exodus 6:3 as Adonai and HERRE, he printed "Iehouah". His table of words at the end of Genesis explains the name: "Iehovah is goddes name/ nether is any creature so called." The same note tells the reader that "as oft as thou seist LORde in great letters", the Hebrew has Iehovah. The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest evidence for Passover, scapegoat, mercy seat, shewbread, Jehovah, taskmaster, birthright, unleavened, network, peace offering and heave offering is this book.

The New Testament of 1526 is the dictionary's earliest source for stumbling-block, busybody, castaway, seashore and jot. The Cologne sheets of 1525 already print jot at Matthew 5:18, "one Iott/ or one tytle of the lawe", with a margin note that explains the word: "Is as moche too saie as the leest letter." The phrases of the 1526 text include "the sygnes of the tymes", which the Cologne sheets also have and where the Wycliffite text has "the tokenes of tymes", and "the powers that be", "eate, drynke and be mery" and "filthy lucre". The 1526 text does not have passover. For the Jewish feast it uses "ester", the English name of the Christian feast at the same season: "after .ij. dayes shalbe ester". Once, at John 18:28, it keeps the Latin word: "that they myght eate Pascha". His revision of 1534 kept "ester" in most places, though it calls the lamb "the paschall lambe" at Matthew 26:17 and John 18:28 and "the pascall lambe" at Mark 14:12. The King James translators of 1611 used passover in the Gospels and left "Easter" once, at Acts 12:4.

Several words in popular lists of Tyndale's coinages are older than he is. The dictionary's earliest evidence for beautiful is from about 1443, for peacemaker from before 1425, for long-suffering from 1496 and for atonement from 1513, in the writing of Thomas More. Some of his best-known phrases first appear in the 1534 revision. The 1526 text has "Blessed are the maynteyners of peace" and "Fyght a good fyght of fayth", and the 1534 text has "Blessed are the peacemakers" and "Fyght the good fyght of fayth". The phrases "salt of the erthe" and "the twynklyng of an i3e" are already in the later Wycliffite version, at Matthew 5:13 and 1 Corinthians 15:52.

The King James translators were told in 1604 to follow the Bishops' Bible, to use "Tindoll's" translation and four others where they agreed "better with the Text", and to keep "the old Ecclesiastical Words", "viz. the Word Church not to be translated Congregation". The 1611 text kept church. Tyndale's "elders", from his revision, and his "repent" stayed as well. In 1998 Jon Nielson and Royal Skousen proposed a method for determining how much of the 1611 text each earlier English version contributed. From eighteen sampled passages, all from the parts of the Bible Tyndale translated, they concluded that his contribution to the King James New Testament is about 84 percent of the text, and that in the Old Testament about 76 percent of his words were retained. Earlier estimates had run from 18 percent to up to 90 percent. The estimate rests on eighteen passages and covers only the parts he translated. The 1611 translators worked from the Bishops' Bible, and Alfred Pollard judged in 1911 that the later Protestant versions of the books Tyndale translated "must be looked upon as revisions of his, not as independent translations."

Tyndale was back in Antwerp by late 1529, and in 1534 he lodged with the English merchant Thomas Poyntz while he revised his New Testament. In the spring of 1535 an Englishman named Henry Phillips arranged his arrest. He was held in the castle of Vilvoorde, north of Brussels. A letter in his hand survives in the archives in Brussels. It asks for a warmer cap, a warmer coat and a lamp in the evening, and above all for his Hebrew Bible, Hebrew grammar and Hebrew dictionary, "ut eo studio tempus conteram", so that he could spend his time in that study. Theologians from Leuven examined him for heresy. He was degraded from the priesthood in August 1536 and then strangled and burned at Vilvoorde. The traditional date is 6 October 1536. A Brabant account book records a payment to the castle's lieutenant for keeping him 501 days, and the historian Paul Arblaster reads it as placing the execution in early September.

In this series, Matteo Ricci and Xu Guangqi built Chinese terms for Euclid from common characters, and Eliezer Ben-Yehuda built Hebrew nouns from roots and patterns. Many of the words first recorded in Tyndale's translations join two plain English words: pass and over, scape and goat, mercy and seat, task and master, birth and right.

If you have the Oxford English Dictionary's full entry for broken-hearted: does its 1526 quotation come from the 1526 text, which at Luke 4:18 reads "to heale them which are troubled in there hertes", or from the revision of 1534, which reads "to heale the broken harted"?

## 5. Fact-check list

**Verified against a primary or scholarly source**
1. The 1408/9 constitution: adopted by the provincial council at Oxford and ratified at St Paul's, London, on 14 January 1408/9; no one to translate scripture into English "on his own authority", and no such translation to be read until "approved by the diocesan of the place or if need be by a provincial council" (Pollard, no. I, pp. 79–81, Latin and translation). Edward Lee's letter of 2 December [1525] cites the "constitutions prouincall" against English Bibles (Pollard, no. VII).
2. Tyndale's movements: born 1491 to 1494, probably in Gloucestershire; BA 1512 at Magdalen Hall, Oxford, and MA 1515 (the timeline says from Oxford University; the Society's FAQ gives 1488 to 1494 for the birth); London 1523; left England 1524; Cologne 1525; Worms 1526; back in Antwerp from late 1529 ("Tyndale's translations and writings"); lodging with Thomas Poyntz from the summer of 1534; arrested in the spring of 1535 through Henry Phillips (Tyndale Society timeline and pages). His own account of Tunstall ("W. T. To the Reader", 1530, Mombert, p. 5): he hoped "to come to this mannes service", brought Sir Harry Gilford, the king's comptroller, "an oration of Isocrates which I had translated out of greke in to English", and was told "his house was full"; he stayed in London "almoste an yere" and found "no rowme in my lorde of londons palace to translate the new testament ... no place to do it in all englonde". The prologue does not say that he asked Tunstall to support a translation.
3. Cologne: three thousand copies in the press, stopped at quire K on Cochlaeus's information through Hermann Rinck and the city council; the two Englishmen took the printed quires up the Rhine to Worms (Cochlaeus 1549, in Pollard, no. VI, translation pp. 107–108; Cochlaeus does not name the two or the printer, and Pollard's introduction, p. 4, names "Tyndale and his amanuensis, William Roy"). Cochlaeus left three accounts, of 1533, 1538 and 1549, "the fullest" being the last (Pollard, p. 4, n. 1). Roye's role: Tyndale, 1528: help "both to write and to helpe me to compare the textes together" (Pollard, no. XII). The surviving Cologne fragment: 31 leaves, prologue and Matthew to the middle of chapter 22, with marginal notes, bequeathed by Thomas Grenville (Pollard, introduction, pp. 4–5).
4. Worms: an octavo, identified as Peter Schoeffer's from its types and illustrations, without the Cologne marginal notes (Pollard, pp. 5–6). Tyndale's name is not on the Stuttgart title page, the only one that survives (Werrell). Tyndale's own reason for leaving his name off: Christ's counsel "to doo theyr good deades secretly" (preface, 1528, Pollard, no. XII). The British Library copy is complete except for the title page and was sold by Bristol Baptist College to the British Library by an agreement of 21 April 1994 (West). Three copies survive (Pollard 1911 names Bristol and St Paul's; the Württembergische Landesbibliothek's catalogue says it identified the Stuttgart copy, the only complete one with a title page, in autumn 1996). The British Library catalogue assigns the edition "to the press of Peter Schoeffer" from its Schwabacher types.
5. Sources of the translation: primarily the Greek, with Luther's German, the Vulgate and Erasmus's Latin as aids (Pollard, pp. 5–6); the Greek text "in so far as Erasmus had been able to compile this" (Tyndale Society, "Tyndale's translations and writings"; also Huntington and Marsh 2011). The 1526 epilogue: "I had no man to counterfet ..." and "a thing begunne rather then fynnesshed" (Offor).
6. Suppression: Tunstall's order, thirty days to surrender all copies (Foxe 1563 in Pollard, no. XV), dated in the Latin "24. die mensis Octobris" 1526 and in Foxe's English "the xxiii. of October" (Pollard, p. 135; the script and post use the Latin date); copies burned at a sermon at St Paul's Cross at the end of October 1526, "a few copies" in Shamir's account, whose details "are not known" (Shamir 2013, pp. 367, 371; Tyndale Society); Tunstall's "more than two thousand" corruptions, which Cochlaeus reports as hearsay ("Ferunt ...", "supra duo Milia deprauationum atque peruersitatum"), in the Latin only (Pollard, p. 103; Pollard's translation leaves the sentence out); Ridley's letter of 24 February [1527] (Pollard, no. XIII); the royal proclamation of June 1530 and the instrument of 24 May 1530 naming "the translation also of Scripture corrupted by William Tyndall, as well in the Olde Testamente as in the Newe" (Pollard, no. XXII).
7. More and Tyndale: More's *Dialogue*, dated 1528 on its title page and published in the summer of 1529, book 3, chapter 8, names "prestys", "chyrch" and "charyte" rendered "senyours", "congregacyon" and "loue" (Pollard, no. XIV, p. 128; Walter 1850). Those are More's spellings; the 1526 print has "seniours" or "senyours", "congregacion" and "love" (Offor). Tyndale's *Answer*, printed about spring 1531, defends "congregation" for *ecclesia* (Parker Society, 1850, p. 13). More's *Confutacyon* printed 1532.
8. The Pentateuch: the Genesis colophon of 17 January 1530 with the false "Malborow" and "Hans Luft"; printed in Antwerp; Genesis and Numbers gothic, Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy roman; Kronenberg (1919) attributed the Hans Luft books to Hoochstraten, Valkema Blouw (1996) to de Keyser and Grapheus (Biblia Sacra, five edition records). Eleven woodcuts of the tabernacle in Exodus, beginning with "The forme of the arke of wittnesse" on fol. **Contested, flag in the post or caption**
16. The date of execution. Traditionally 6 October 1536. Arblaster argues that "the traditional commemoration of Tyndale's execution on 6 October has no basis", explains it as Foxe listing Tyndale in his calendar "as the sixth martyr whom he thought had died in the month of October", notes that Hall and Bale put the execution in September (of 1535, "a year early"), and, from 501 days of keep and an arrest during the Bergen op Zoom fair (probably 24 April 1535), places it on about 6 September 1536. The Tyndale Society gives "late September or early October, 1536", and Daniell, as quoted by Wikipedia, "one of the first days of October 1536". The script says only "in 1536".
17. The date of the arrest. The Tyndale Society says "the spring of 1535". Arblaster argues that it fell while the English merchants were at the Bergen op Zoom fair, between Easter (28 March) and 15 May 1535, probably on 24 April, so that any later May date is too late. Dates in late May given elsewhere were not checked. The script says only "in 1535".
18. The date of the Oxford council: Pollard gives 1408, with ratification in London on 14 January 1408/9. Nicholas Watson's standard study is titled "Censorship and Cultural Change in Late-Medieval England: Vernacular Theology, the Oxford Translation Debate, and Arundel's Constitutions of 1409" (*Speculum* 70, no. 4, 1995: 822–864, doi:10.2307/2865345; the article was not opened), and modern scholarship dates the Oxford meeting to 1407: "The Oxford constitutions were passed in November 1407" and confirmed in London in January 1409 (Kelly 2016, chapter 5), and Powell (2014) and Forrest (2013) give 1407 for the Oxford meeting and 1409 for the London one. Pollard's "1408" is therefore probably a year late. The script and captions use the London ratification, January 1409, which every source agrees on; the post says "adopted by a church council at Oxford" with no year.
19. The printer of the 1530 Pentateuch: Hoochstraten (Kronenberg 1919, STC) or de Keyser and Grapheus (Valkema Blouw 1996). Caption it "printed in Antwerp" with no printer's name.
20. How independent the Pentateuch is of Luther. Slater (1906): a first draft from the German, checked against the Hebrew. Hammond (1980) reopened the question; his conclusion was not read. The script says only that Tyndale learned Hebrew and that some words follow Luther's.
21. The King James figure. Its limits: eighteen passages; only the books Tyndale translated (for the Old Testament these are usually taken to include Joshua to 2 Chronicles, first printed in Matthew's Bible of 1537, which Pollard says "has been conjectured with every appearance of reason to be Tyndale's continuation of his translation"; the abstract does not list the books); the 76 percent is phrased in the abstract as Tyndale's words "retained", the 84 percent as his "contribution ... of the text", and the two may not be the same measure, so the caption and the post now give each in the abstract's terms; the method beyond the abstract was not read, so the brief states no detail of it (how passages were chosen, how words were counted or matched). That the later Bibles carried his wording to 1611 is Pollard's judgment, not the study's ("must be looked upon as revisions of his", Pollard, p. 6). Popular sources also give "83 percent": Daniell's *The Bible in English* (2003), p. 448, as cited by Wikipedia, and Juhász (2010), as a search snippet, report 83 percent, the second as the figure for the whole Bible; neither was opened. Daniell's "nine-tenths" (via Mansbridge) is another figure. Use 84 and 76 with the citation, never 83 or 90. Gergely Juhász's chapter (*The King James Bible after Four Hundred Years*, Cambridge, 2010, pp. 100–123) is reported in a search snippet to call the figures "only indicative" because the sample is small and the passages were chosen for their familiarity; the chapter was not opened (academia.edu returned HTTP 403), so this criticism stays out of the film.
22. The ploughboy saying. First printed by Foxe in 1563 (p. 570), 27 years after Tyndale's death ("nearly" thirty, in Martin's words), with no definitive source; the idea appears in Erasmus's *Paraclesis* of 1516 (Martin 2016). Not used in the script; if used, attribute it to Foxe.

24. OED "broken-hearted": the OED's earliest evidence is dated 1526, Tyndale, but the 1526 text reads "troubled in there hertes" at Luke 4:18, and "broken harted" is first found in the 1534 revision. The OED quotation was not seen. Likewise "two-edged" (OED 1526) has a Wycliffite antecedent, the compound "tweyne eggid" (twain-edged) at Hebrews 4:12, and "sin-offering", "trespass-offering" and "meat offering" (OED 1535) are already in the 1530 Pentateuch as printed by Mombert. All three are on the original leaves of the Princeton copy (Leviticus 7:37, canvas 390: "of burntoffrynges, of meatoffrynges, of synne offrynges, of trespaceoffrynges"; Leviticus 16, canvas 424: "synne offrynge", "synneofferynge"). The OED quotations were not seen, so call them possible antedatings. The earliest places in the 1530 text are Exodus 29:14, "For it is a synneofferynge", and Exodus 29:41, "the meateoffrynge" (Mombert). In the same way "jot" (OED 1526) is already in the Cologne sheets of 1525, "one *Iott/ or one tytle of the lawe", with a margin note that glosses it (Arber's facsimile, p. 24), and so are "the sygnes of the tymes" and "my cōgregacion" (p. 50). The Cologne quarto was stopped in the press, and whether its sheets were ever issued is not known (Pollard, p. 5), so the brief keeps 1526 as the book these words are printed in and names the Cologne sheets as the first printing.
