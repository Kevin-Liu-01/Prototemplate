# Ḥunayn, Ḥubaysh, and the Arabic vocabulary of Galen

Arabic series, installment: Ḥunayn's *Risāla* on the Galen translations, رسالة حنين بن إسحق إلى علي بن يحيى في ذكر ما ترجم من كتب جالينوس بعلمه وبعض ما لم يترجم, written 855–856.

The anchor is the record of the translation work. It holds the place that the 1908 dictionary holds in the Hebrew series: a book whose own text records how the words were made. Ḥunayn's letter lists Galen's books, who translated each one, for whom, and how. The vocabulary comes from a second book of his, the *Ten Treatises on the Eye*. It names each coat of the eye in Greek and in Arabic, and its Latin version renders the Arabic names as retina, uvea and cornea.

## 2. The post (single long-form post, no reply chain)

33.35° N, 44.34° E · Baghdad

Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (حنين بن إسحق) translated the medical works of the Greek physician Galen into Syriac and Arabic in ninth-century Baghdad. Most sources date his life from about 808 to 873. He came from the ʿIbād, the Arab Christian community of al-Ḥīra in southern Iraq. He belonged to the Church of the East. He probably grew up with Arabic and Syriac, and he learned Greek as a young man. The medieval biographers disagree about where he learned it: Alexandria or Byzantine territory.

The best record of his work is a letter he wrote in 855 or 856, when he was 48. He wrote it for ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā, a secretary at the court of the caliph al-Mutawakkil. Its title promises an account of "the books of Galen that have been translated, to his knowledge, and some that have not." He wrote it from memory, because he had lost every book he had collected in his life. The letter lists 129 works of Galen. For most of them it gives:

- the number of books and their contents;
- the earlier translations and their quality;
- Ḥunayn's own Syriac or Arabic version;
- the person the version was made for.

Ḥunayn added his newer translations to the letter in 864. By Max Meyerhof's count, the list credits Ḥunayn with 95 Syriac and 39 Arabic versions. It credits his pupils with about 70 Arabic versions, most of which he revised. Gotthelf Bergsträsser edited the letter in 1925 from a manuscript in the Aya Sofya library in Istanbul and translated it into German; the count of 129 is the number of entries in his edition. A second version of the letter survives in another Aya Sofya manuscript. John C. Lamoreaux edited that version with an English translation in 2016, with his own section numbers and Bergsträsser's in the margin.

The letter describes his method. Ḥunayn translated Galen's *On Sects for Beginners* into Syriac at "twenty or a little more", for a physician of Jundishapur. He worked from a single Greek manuscript with many omissions. When he was about forty, his pupil Ḥubaysh asked him to correct the translation. By then Ḥunayn had several Greek manuscripts of the book. He collated them against one another until one correct copy came out of them. Then he collated his Syriac against that copy and corrected it. "This is my habit in everything I translate," he writes. A few years later he translated the book into Arabic for Muḥammad ibn Mūsā, one of three brothers who paid for many translations.

Other entries show how much work the method took. The sixth-century translator Sergius of Rēsh ʿAynā had put Galen's *Therapeutic Method* into Syriac, and the physician Salmawayh asked Ḥunayn to correct the second half. Salmawayh held the Syriac and read it aloud, and Ḥunayn held the Greek. Each time the two differed, Ḥunayn told him. Partway through one book, Salmawayh decided that a new translation would be easier and better, and Ḥunayn made one. That was in al-Raqqa, during al-Maʾmūn's campaigns.

The new translation was sent down to Baghdad to be copied. A fire broke out in the vessel carrying it, and no copy survived. Years later Ḥunayn translated the whole work again for the physician Bukhtīshūʿ ibn Jibrīl. For the last eight books he now had a number of Greek manuscripts, and he collated them into one correct copy. For the first six books he had found only one very faulty manuscript, and later a second. He wrote that he would collate a third if one came his way.

He looked for Galen's *On Demonstration* in Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt, as far as Alexandria. He found about half of it, in incomplete parts, in Damascus. The Greek text of that book is now lost.

Ḥunayn worked with his family and his pupils. His son Isḥāq translated mainly Greek philosophy, above all Aristotle. Isḥāq died in 910. His nephew Ḥubaysh ibn al-Ḥasan al-Aʿsam was the son of Ḥunayn's sister. Ḥubaysh and Ḥunayn's pupil ʿĪsā ibn Yaḥyā made many of the Arabic versions. A translation usually went through two steps:

1. Ḥunayn put the Greek into Syriac, often for a Christian physician.
2. Ḥubaysh or ʿĪsā put the Syriac into Arabic, often for a Muslim patron.

Ḥunayn then revised some of the Arabic. Ḥubaysh suggested that Ḥunayn collect his separate treatises on the eye into one book. After Ḥunayn's death, Ḥubaysh completed his introduction to medicine, the *Questions on Medicine for Students*.

The names on the manuscripts do not always match the translators. In 987 the bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm wrote that "among Ḥunayn's good fortunes" was this: the Arabic translations of Ḥubaysh, ʿĪsā ibn Yaḥyā and others were ascribed to Ḥunayn. In the thirteenth century the historian Ibn al-Qifṭī added a story. He wrote that readers who found Ḥubaysh's name on an old translation often thought that a copyist had miswritten Ḥunayn's. Those readers scraped the name off and wrote Ḥunayn's. In Arabic script the two names, حبيش and حنين, begin with the same three letter shapes. They differ in their dots and in their last letter.

The *Ten Treatises on the Eye* shows how the Arabic medical vocabulary was made. Ḥunayn wrote its parts over more than thirty years, and Meyerhof called the collection the earliest surviving systematic textbook of ophthalmology. Ḥunayn calls the lens the ice-like humour (al-ruṭūba al-jalīdiyya). The first treatise places it between two other humours: a glass-like humour behind it and an egg-white-like humour in front. Three coats lie behind these and three in front. For each part Ḥunayn gives the Greek name in Arabic letters, followed by an Arabic name that translates it:

- The net-like coat is amphiblēstroeidēs in Greek and al-shabakiyya in Arabic.
- The coat like the membrane around a fetus is chorioeidēs in Greek. Ḥunayn calls it al-mashīmiyya, from mashīma, the afterbirth.
- The grape-like coat is rhagoeidēs in Greek and al-ʿinabiyya in Arabic.
- The horn-like coat is keratoeidēs in Greek and al-qarniyya in Arabic.
- The outer membrane is epipephykōs, "grown upon," in Greek, and al-multaḥim, "the one that adheres," in Arabic.

Ḥunayn notes that some authorities count seven coats and others six, five, four, three or two. He adds that they disagree only about which parts to call coats.

Meyerhof's two manuscripts were copied in 1156 and 1196, and both go back to a copy dated 1003. Only the copy of 1196 has the diagrams that the text introduces with the words "and it is like this figure." Meyerhof printed the book as "ascribed to" Ḥunayn. Bergsträsser had judged that its surviving Arabic was not Ḥunayn's own in its present form and might have been changed by Ḥubaysh and other pupils. Meyerhof took the original edition to be Ḥunayn's and the final redaction to be his pupils'.

Constantine the African was a monk of Monte Cassino. In the late eleventh century he translated nine of the ten treatises into Latin and issued them as his own *Liber de oculis*. An otherwise unknown Demetrius made a second Latin version, and it was printed among the works of Galen. Julius Hirschberg showed in 1903 that both Latin books are Ḥunayn's.

Constantine's Latin keeps Ḥunayn's pattern of a Greek name followed by a translated one. The Lyon print of 1515 also keeps both of Ḥunayn's comparisons for the thin membrane over the lens. The Arabic says it is "like the skin of an onion and the web of a spider", and the print says "sicut tenuissimum cepe vel aranee tela", like the finest onion or a spider's web. The Codex Gigas has rete, "net", where the print has cepe. That manuscript was written in Bohemia between 1204 and 1230, and its copy of the *Liber de oculis* reads:

- The first coat behind the vitreous "surrounds it like a net, and so it is called retina."
- The second "resembles the afterbirth, and it is called secundina."
- The grape-like coat is "ragaidos, that is, uvea," from the Latin uva, a grape.
- The horn-like coat is "keraidos, that is, cornea."

The Codex Gigas and the Lyon print of 1515 both list the seven coats as retina, secundina, scliros, aranee tela ("spider's web"), uvea, cornea and coniunctiva.

English took retina, uvea and cornea from medieval Latin, and the Latin coniunctiva is already in these medieval translations from Arabic. The English names choroid, sclera and arachnoid came later, through the New Latin of the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries, from Greek. Before sclera, English used sclerotic, from the medieval Latin sclerotica. The historian of ophthalmology Julius Hirschberg wrote in 1908 that the modern names of the coats and humours of the eye come less from the Greeks than from the Arabs, through the medieval Latin translations. One of the Latin names follows the Arabic where the Arabic departs from the Greek: coniunctiva means "joined", as al-multaḥim does, and the Greek epipephykōs means "grown upon".

Ḥunayn's introduction to medicine also reached Latin. His *Questions on Medicine for Students* (al-Masāʾil fī al-ṭibb lil-mutaʿallimīn) became the *Isagoge* of Johannitius, the Latin name for Ḥunayn. It stands first in the Articella, a collection of short medical texts that the US National Library of Medicine calls "the most popular textbook of the late Middle Ages". Its chapter on the eye names the same seven coats as the *Liber de oculis*, beginning "prima tunica dicitur retina" ("the first coat is called retina"). The chapter reads this way in the Codex Gigas (f. 240ra) and in the Venice Articella of 1483 (fol. 2v).

The Isagoge's first lines divide the theory of medicine into three studies: things natural, things not natural and things contrary to nature. The Arabic book divides it differently, into the study of the natural things, of the causes and of the signs, and it sets out the triad later in the book. Both list the seven natural things in the same order. Each Latin term renders an Arabic one:

- elementa: arkān (elements)
- commixtiones: amzija (temperaments)
- compositiones: akhlāṭ (humours)
- membra: aʿḍāʾ (organs)
- virtutes: quwā (faculties)
- operationes: afʿāl (actions)
- spiritus: arwāḥ (spirits)

The Latin sentence "commixtiones sunt novem: octo inequales et una equalis" translates the Arabic sentence that gives nine temperaments, eight unbalanced and one balanced. The things not natural became the "six non-naturals" of later European medicine. Galen had grouped the same six as "necessary causes" in his *Art of Medicine*. In his order they are:

- contact with the surrounding air
- motion and rest
- sleep and waking
- what is taken in
- what is expelled or retained
- what happens to the soul

Some of the Arabic terms translate Greek words directly:

- **Mizāj**, "mixture", renders Galen's krasis. Galen's *On Temperaments* is *Kitāb al-mizāj* in Arabic.
- **Akhlāṭ**, "mixtures", renders chymoi, the humours. The Latin translator of the Isagoge rendered it as compositiones.
- **Buḥrān**, the crisis of a disease, renders krisis in Ḥunayn's translation of Hippocrates. It is also in the Arabic title of Galen's *On Critical Days*. The word passed into Persian and Turkish, where bohrān and buhran now mean a crisis of any kind.

For the elements, Arabic texts of the period use three words: usṭuqus, a transcription of the Greek stoicheion; rukn, a pillar; and ʿunṣur, an origin. In the *Encyclopaedia of Islam*, Gotthard Strohmaier credits Ḥunayn and his school with transcribing Greek terms as rarely as they could. He writes that they helped to form the Arabic scientific vocabulary in this way.

Popular histories describe the organization of this work differently. A common version says that the caliph al-Maʾmūn put Ḥunayn in charge of the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Ḥikma), an academy for translation. In that version the caliph paid him the weight of his books in gold. The medieval sources say less:

- **Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa**, writing in the thirteenth century, reports that al-Maʾmūn "is said to have paid Ḥunayn in gold, weight for weight." He also reports that the caliph summoned Ḥunayn when he was "no more than a youth."
- **Ibn Juljul** was a tenth-century physician, and Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa quotes him. He says that the later caliph al-Mutawakkil put Ḥunayn over a staff of translators and that Ḥunayn corrected their drafts.

Neither names the House of Wisdom as Ḥunayn's post. Ḥunayn's own letter names the people he translated for: physicians such as Jibrīl ibn Bukhtīshūʿ and Salmawayh, the brothers Muḥammad and Aḥmad ibn Mūsā, and officials such as ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā. It never mentions the House of Wisdom. It names al-Maʾmūn once, to date the translation made in al-Raqqa. Strohmaier called this silence remarkable. He concluded that the whole activity "seems to have been based on a kind of private enterprise."

In *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (1998), Dimitri Gutas reads the evidence the same way and goes further:

- He describes the Bayt al-Ḥikma as a library, most likely set up under al-Manṣūr as a bureau of an ʿAbbāsid administration modelled on the Sasanian one. In his account it housed the translation from Persian into Arabic of Sasanian history and culture, and kept the books that resulted. He adds that under al-Maʾmūn it appears to have taken on astronomical and mathematical work as well.
- Among the many reports of translation from Greek, he finds none that mentions it.
- He points out that Ḥunayn describes his searches for Greek manuscripts and never says that he looked for any there.
- He rejects the idea that it was an academy or a meeting place for scholars.
- In his account, the whole ʿAbbāsid elite supported the translation movement, from caliphs and princes to civil servants, military leaders, merchants, bankers, scholars and scientists.

Other historians accept parts of this account and dispute others:

- **Marie-Geneviève Balty-Guesdon** made a more cautious case along the same lines in 1992.
- **Marco Di Branco** (2012) finds no firm evidence for a Sasanian origin. He points to two passages in Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* that Gutas's book does not discuss. One lists Salm, the head of the Bayt al-Ḥikma, among the envoys al-Maʾmūn sent to the Byzantine emperor for Greek books. In the other, the Barmakid vizier Yaḥyā ibn Khālid has Salm and Abū Ḥassān check the translators' work on Ptolemy's *Almagest*. Di Branco notes that the *Encyclopaedia of Islam THREE* entry by Gutas and Kevin van Bladel calls the *Almagest* account fictitious. He concludes that people who worked in the Bayt al-Ḥikma took part in the translation from Greek.
- **Ekmeleddin İhsanoğlu** (2023) traces the modern picture of a grand academy to European scholarship and to mistranslations of Arabic sources.

None of the medieval sources checked for this post puts Ḥunayn at the head of the Bayt al-Ḥikma.

In the Chinese series, Matteo Ricci and Xu Guangqi printed their method at the head of their Euclid: one of them translated by mouth and the other wrote with the brush. Ḥunayn's letter records a method with two steps of its own, from Greek into Syriac and from Syriac into Arabic. For most books it names the person who took each step. Like the Hebrew coiners in this series, his school made technical terms from words every reader already knew. The Hebrew words were built from roots and patterns. Ḥunayn's translated Greek names that were themselves taken from common things: a net, a grape, a horn, ice, glass and the white of an egg.

If you have worked with the Latin manuscripts of Constantine's *Liber de oculis*, two questions remain. Do copies older than the Codex Gigas already call the net-like coat retina? Did thirteenth-century anatomists take the word from this book, from the Latin *Isagoge*, which lists the same seven coats, or from Gerard of Cremona's translations of Avicenna?

## 5. Fact-check list

**Verified against a primary or scholarly source**

*Life and the letter*

1. **The letter's dates.** Ḥunayn wrote it at 48, "in the year 1167 of the years of Alexander", which is 855–856. He added to it in Adar 1175, which is March 864 (Bergsträsser 1925: closing note, German p. 43; introduction, pp. ix–x, where the manuscript's 1195 is corrected). The letter was written after the loss of his whole library, from memory (opening, German pp. 1–3: "daß mein Gedächtnis nicht ausreicht" and "was ich in betreff jener Bücher auswendig weiß"; EI2).
2. **Life dates.**
   - Ḥunayn died on Tuesday 6 Ṣafar 260, which is 1 Kānūn I 1185 of Alexander, or 1 December 873 (Ibn al-Qifṭī; Bergsträsser p. ix n. 1, citing the *Fihrist*).
   - EI2 gives 192/808 to 260/873 and al-Ḥīra as his birthplace. DSB gives 808 to 873.
3. **Background.** The ʿIbād of al-Ḥīra (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29.1). The Church of the East: EI2 says "the Syrian Nestorian church". His father was a pharmacist (EI2; DSB); Yūsuf ibn Ibrāhīm's account in Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29.2 says instead that he "came of a family of money-changers in al-Ḥīrah", so keep the father's trade out of the film. Arabic and Syriac from childhood is EI2's inference ("may be assumed to have been bilingual from his youth"), so the post says "probably".
4. **The letter's contents.**
   - It lists 129 numbered entries (Bergsträsser 1925; no. 129 ends on German p. 42). Not every entry records a translation: for nos. 22 and 23, the abridgments of Marinus and Lycus, Ḥunayn says he has never seen the book (Arabic p. 20; German p. 16). The script therefore says the letter tells "which ones had been translated, and by whom". Bracketed passages in the 1925 text are additions by a later hand; Meyerhof calls the list "completed by one of his pupils or friends" (p. xxiii).
   - The only manuscript of 1925 is Aya Sofya 3631 (Bergsträsser, introduction). Meyerhof names Aya Sofya 3631 and 3590 (p. xxiii).
   - Meyerhof's count is 95 Syriac and 39 Arabic versions by Ḥunayn and about 70 Arabic versions by his pupils, mostly revised by him (p. xxiii).
   - EI2 gives "129 titles, of which he himself translated about 100".

*The method*

5. **No. 3, *On Sects for Beginners*.** Translated at "twenty or a little more" for Shīrīshūʿ ibn Quṭrub of Jundishapur, from a Greek copy "كثيرة الأسقاط". At about forty, at Ḥubaysh's request, he collated several copies into one correct copy and corrected his Syriac against it, adding "وكذلك من عادتي أن أفعل في جميع ما أترجمه". He translated it into Arabic for Muḥammad ibn Mūsā a few years later (Arabic p. 5, ll. 1–9, read on the scan; German p. 4). The print spells the town "جندي سابور" in two words, and "نسخة يونانية كثيرة الأسقاط" is on l. 3.
6. **No. 20, *Therapeutic Method*.**
   - Sergius's Syriac; Salmawayh reading the Syriac against Ḥunayn's Greek; the decision to retranslate.
   - "Wir waren (damals) in Raqqa zur Zeit der Feldzüge des Ma'mūn."
   - The fire in the vessel carrying the copy to Baghdad, and the later retranslation for Bukhtīshūʿ ibn Jibrīl.
   - "Eine Anzahl Handschriften" of books 7–14; one very faulty manuscript, then a second, of books 1–6; the wish for a third (German pp. 13–14).
   - Salmawayh and Ḥunayn collated "ein Stück des siebenten Teils" before Salmawayh gave up on correcting (German p. 14).
   - The vessel: Bergsträsser's German says "Fahrzeug", but his Arabic text says "فوقع حريق في السفينة التي كان فيها زكريا", a fire in the ship that Zakariyyā was on (Arabic p. 18, l. 5). "Boat" is the source's word, not an inference.
7. **No. 115, *On Demonstration*.** The search through Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt as far as Alexandria, with about half found in Damascus in incomplete parts (German p. 39; Arabic pp. 47–48). The Greek is lost (Havrda and Koetschet 2025, record).
8. **No. 13.** His first translation of this book, into Syriac at about 17, for Jibrīl ibn Bukhtīshūʿ, with only one translation before it (German p. 9).

*The team*

9. **Isḥāq.** Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn died in Baghdad in Rabīʿ II 298, December 910, and translated mainly philosophy (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29.8, 8.30).
10. **Ḥubaysh and ʿĪsā.**
    - Ḥubaysh was the son of Ḥunayn's sister (8.31).
    - He completed the *Masāʾil* (8.29 list no. 1, quoting Ibn Abī Ṣādiq: Ḥunayn made a final copy of only part of it in his lifetime, and Ḥubaysh edited the rest and added to it; 8.31; Ibn al-Qifṭī, "وزاد فيها حبيش الأعسم تلميذه"; Meyerhof p. xxviii). The note on BL Arundel Or 10, f. 28r is a different claim: see item 33a.
    - He proposed collecting the eye treatises into one book (8.29 list no. 2).
    - Ḥubaysh and ʿĪsā made Arabic versions from Ḥunayn's Syriac much more often than the reverse (EI2).
    - Ḥunayn translated into Syriac for Christian colleagues and into Arabic for Muslim sponsors (EI2).
11. **The names on the manuscripts.**
    - The *Fihrist* (Tajaddud p. 348): "من سعادات حنين، أن ما نقله حبيش بن الحسن الأعسم، وعيسى بن يحيى، وغيرهما إلى العربي، ينحل إلى حنين". It adds that Ḥunayn translated mostly into Syriac.
    - Ibn al-Qifṭī: readers took Ḥubaysh's name for a miswriting, "فيكشطه ويجعله لحنين". EI2: colophons usually name Ḥunayn alone.

*The vocabulary*

12. **The *Ten Treatises*.**
    - The Greek and Arabic names of the humours and coats (Meyerhof, Arabic pp. 74–75, 80; English pp. 4–5, 10–11; all three Arabic pages read on the scan in checking pass 1). On p. 74 the choroid's Greek name is Meyerhof's restoration; both manuscripts repeat the grape-like coat's name there (English p. 4 n. 1).
    - The remark on the authorities who count seven to two coats: "والاختلاف بينهم لا في المعنى بل في اللفظ" (Arabic p. 80, l. 9; Meyerhof's English p. 10: "The difference between them is one in term only and not in meaning"). The next lines show that the disagreement is over which parts to call coats.
    - The figure introduced by "على هذا المثال" (Arabic p. 75; Plate 1).
    - The parts were written over "thirty-odd years" (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29 list no. 2).
    - The manuscripts: L, 551/1156; C, 592/1196, with the five diagrams, which L lacks; C was copied from a manuscript in the hand of L's copyist, collated with a copy going back to one dated 394/1003, and Meyerhof concludes that both depend on that old manuscript (pp. xlvii–xlix).
    - "The earliest existing systematic text-book of ophthalmology" is Meyerhof's subtitle.
    - The authorship hedge: Meyerhof titles the book "ascribed to" Ḥunayn because Bergsträsser judged its surviving Arabic too bad to be Ḥunayn's own and possibly changed by Ḥubaysh and other pupils; "the original edition is his, but the final redaction that of his pupils" (pp. xlix–l). See item 33b.
13. **The Latin versions.**
    - Constantine's *Liber de oculis* has nine treatises, issued under his own name and dedicated to his pupil Johannes. The Demetrius version has ten and was printed with Galen. Hirschberg identified both in 1903 (Meyerhof pp. vii–viii, xxxiv–xxxv).
    - The Codex Gigas readings, f. 249ra, read again on the full-resolution image in checking pass 1: the rubric "Prohemium Constantini monachi Cassinensis"; the prologue "idcirco ego constantinus cassinensis monachus ... hunc libellum composui" (the words between are probably "tibi Iohanni"); the rubric "Incipit liber de natura oculorum"; "quorum primus eam circumdat quasi rete, unde dicitur retina"; "similatur secundine, et dicitur secundina"; "et dicitur scliros"; "dicitur geloidos .i. vitrea"; "vocaturque euagaidos"; "unde vocant eum ragaidos .i. uvea"; "cornui similis, dicitur keraidos .i. cornea"; and the cataract as water, "Aqua cum inter cristalleidos et spiritum steterit". The manuscript abbreviates; the forms here are expanded.
    - Column b of the same page: "sic[ut] tenuissimu[m] rete [vel] aranee tela"; the list of seven, "retinam, secundinam, scliros, aranee telam, uveam, corneam et coniunctivam"; and "panniculus autem exterior .i. coniunctiva".
    - The Lyon 1515 readings, fols. 172r–v, read again on leaves n794–n795: "quasi rete unde dicitur retina", "hyalaidos .i. vitrea", "ragaydos .i. vuea", "keraydos idest cornea", the list of seven, and "sicut tenuissimum cepe vel aranee tela" (the print has tenuissimū, so the brief's earlier "tenuissima" was wrong). The phrase matches Ḥunayn's "شبيه بقشر البصلة وبنسج العنكبوت" (Meyerhof, Arabic p. 80); the Codex Gigas has rete in place of cepe.
    - The Latin *Isagoge* lists the same coats: "Oculorum tunice sunt septem et humores tres. Prima tunica dicitur retina, secunda secundina, tertia scliros, quarta tela aranee, quinta uvea, sexta cornea, septima coniunctiva" (*Articella* 1483, fol. 2v, BIU leaf n7), and the Codex Gigas copy of the *Isagoge* reads the same to "sexta cor[nea]" (f. 240ra, with "quarta aranea").
    - The Codex Gigas date ("between 1204 and 1230") and shelfmark come from the IIIF manifest; f. 249r is canvas `ms-100500_0495` (6380 × 10400 px) and f. 240r is `ms-100500_0477` (6460 × 10400 px).
14. **French dates.** From TLFi, rechecked in checking pass 1 through the CNRTL data:
    - rétine: 1314 (Mondeville); Latin retina c. 1250, after Latham. Gerard of Cremona's twelfth-century translations already have retina (Hirschberg 1908, p. 155).
    - cornée: 1314; Latin cornea 1267.
    - uvée: 1495, in the sense of the choroid (Bernard de Gordon, via Godefroy); the DMF has the eye sense in 1492.
    - conjonctive: c. 1370 (Chauliac); the DMF has 1362–65 and a thirteenth-century attestation in the French Albucasis.
    - cataracte (eye): c. 1340 (Machaut, a poem); Latin cataracta medical since the sixth century, after Gregory of Tours in TLL.
    - sclérotique: 1314, from medieval Latin sclerotica, before 1250 (Latham); choroïde 1538; arachnoïde 1538/1541, for a coat of the eye in Paré, 1575.
15. **The *Isagoge* and the *Masāʾil*.**
    - The 1483 *Articella*, fol. 2r, as read (again in checking pass 1, BIU leaf n6): the incipit, the three divisions, the seven natural things, "commixtiones sunt novem", "compositiones sunt quattuor", and the threefold virtus.
    - The *Masāʾil* parallels, now read on the page images of the 1978 edition: the seven natural things in the same order (p. 2) and the nine temperaments (p. 4). The three divisions do not open the Arabic book. Its first page divides the study (النظر) into the natural things, the causes and the signs (p. 1); the triad of natural, non-natural and contra-natural things comes later (p. 214, the things that change the pulse, and pp. 226–227). The earlier brief's "the three divisions" as a parallel was wrong.
    - The *Isagoge* is "another recension" of the *Masāʾil* (EI2, after Steinschneider). Jacquart 1986 (Persée summary) calls it a close patchwork of literally translated fragments of the *Masāʾil*. Meyerhof notes separate printings at Leipzig in 1497 and Strasbourg in 1534 (p. xxviii); it had already been printed within the *Articella*, from Padua about 1476.
    - NLM: "The Articella was the most popular textbook of the late Middle Ages" (verbatim on the exhibition page).
16. **Greek–Arabic pairs.**
    - GlossGA: κρίσις–بحران in Ḥunayn's *Aphorisms* II 12; κρᾶσις–مزاج in Galen, *Quod animi virtutes*, translated by Ḥubaysh; κρυσταλλοειδής–جليدي in Ps.-Plutarch, translated by Qusṭā ibn Lūqā. Only the first is Ḥunayn's own translation.
    - Galen's book titles in Arabic: *Kitāb al-mizāj* (no. 12, "كتابه في المزاج", Arabic p. 10; Add MS 23407), *Kitāb ayyām al-buḥrān* (no. 19, Arabic p. 16; *Fihrist* p. 348, "نقل حنين"; Or 6670 colophon), and *On Anatomical Procedures* as "علاج التشريح" in the *Risāla* (no. 21, Arabic p. 19) and "عمل التشريح" in the manuscripts that credit the Arabic to Ḥunayn (Add MS 23406; Yale Cushing Arabic Ms. 8). The *Risāla*'s entry for no. 21 names no Arabic translator, and Garofalo's edition calls the Arabic "Hunaino Isaaci filio ascripta".
    - EI2: Ḥunayn's school "avoided mere transcriptions as far as possible, and thus helped to forge the Arabic scientific terminology".
17. **The six non-naturals.** Galen's six "necessary causes" in the *Art of Medicine* (Kühn I 367), in the order the post gives, and οὐ φύσει in *On the Pulse for Beginners* (Kühn VIII 462, 470), as summarized in Agerholm 2013, a work-in-progress conference paper, after Niebyl 1971. The *Masāʾil* (1978 ed., pp. 226–227) also counts six "الأمور التي ليست بطبيعية", but its list is the surrounding air, movement and rest, bathing, food and drink, sleep and waking and evacuation, retention and intercourse, and it says some added the events of the soul; the manuscripts vary. The post therefore ties "the same six" to Galen and the Latin tradition only.

*The House of Wisdom*

18. **The letter's silence.**
    - The letter names al-Maʾmūn once, in the Raqqa dating of no. 20 ("وكنا بالرقة في أيام غزوات المأمون", Arabic p. 18, l. 3, read on the scan), and mentions no House of Wisdom (Bergsträsser's index; a search of the German OCR finds "Weisheit" only for the Creator's wisdom in no. 49).
    - EI2: "It is remarkable that there is no word about the famous bayt al-hikma; the whole activity seems to have been based on a kind of private enterprise."
19. **Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, 8.29.5.**
    - "Al-Maʾmūn is said to have paid Ḥunayn in gold, weight for weight."
    - Ḥunayn was summoned when "no more than a youth" ("al-Maʾmūn had Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq sent for. He was no more than a youth at the time"), and asked to translate Greek philosophy and revise other translators.
    - The same chapter (8.29.6) says Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa saw books in the hand of Ḥunayn's secretary al-Azraq, with Ḥunayn's Greek notes, that "bore the seal of al-Maʾmūn". Not used; it bears on the caliphal library question.
    - "Salm, the superintendent of the Bayt al-ḥikmah", was among the envoys sent to Byzantium.
    - The Banū Shākir paid translators 500 dinars a month, after al-Sijistānī.
    - Ibn Juljul (8.29.9): al-Mutawakkil put Ḥunayn over a staff of translators, among them Iṣṭifan ibn Basīl and Mūsā ibn Khālid; no House of Wisdom is named.
20. **Gutas's position.** Via Di Branco 2012 (Italian ed. pp. 65–71) and, in checking pass 1, via verbatim quotations of the English (pp. 1–2, 54, 58–60; see section 4 and `research/readings.md` §7):
    - It was "a library, most likely established as a 'bureau' under al Mansur", part of an administration on the Sasanian model, housing translations from Persian of Sasanian history and culture; under al-Maʾmūn it seems to have gained astronomical and mathematical work (pp. 58–59).
    - The only *Fihrist* passages tying it to translation concern Persian.
    - None of the dozens of reports of Greek translation mentions it (p. 59).
    - Ḥunayn never says he looked for manuscripts there (p. 59).
    - It was "certainly also not an 'academy'", nor a meeting place for scholars (p. 59).
    - The movement was "supported by the entire elite of 'Abbasid society" (pp. 1–2).
    - The word "palace" is not in these passages; see item 34.
    - D'Ancona (SEP, §3, verbatim): the court library "cannot be viewed as an institutional center for the translations of Greek works into Arabic"; the same sentence goes on to say that al-Kindī's circle was nonetheless close to the court.
21. **Di Branco 2012** (pp. 255–263, read in full). No hard evidence for a Sasanian origin (abstract; §1). Gutas's book passes over two *Fihrist* passages (§§2–3, appendices 1 and 3): the envoys to the Byzantine emperor, including "سلماً صاحب بيت الحكمة" (p. 243 Flügel, p. 304 Tajaddud), and the *Almagest*, which Yaḥyā ibn Khālid had Abū Ḥassān and "سلم صاحب بيت الحكمة" improve after testing the best translators (pp. 267–268 Flügel, p. 327 Tajaddud). The *Fihrist* also says Salm worked with Sahl ibn Hārūn and translated from Persian (p. 120 Flügel, p. 134 Tajaddud). Di Branco's own view is that the Bayt al-Ḥikma began as a palace library on the general Umayyad and Byzantine model, and that some of its staff took part in translation from Greek. He reports (n. 45) that Gutas and van Bladel's EI3 entry calls the *Almagest* story "fictitious". Balty-Guesdon's theses were "much more cautious" (pp. 255–256, n. 11).
22. **The popular claim.** Tschanz 2003, p. 39, writes that in 830 Ḥunayn "was placed in charge of the Bayt al Hikmah" and was paid for his translations by their weight in gold (both sentences checked verbatim). The essay has ten general references and cites none for these claims. English Wikipedia repeats the claim from Tschanz and notes Gougenheim's denial.
23. **Vagelpohl on the method.**
    - Vagelpohl 2011: "An examination of his extant translations confirms most of his claims, however transparently self-promoting they often read."
    - Vagelpohl 2010: Ḥunayn's comments are concerned with "making the ideas of a source text understood in the target language". Al-Ṣafadī's claims "have been criticised on numerous occasions, e.g. by Endress (1973, p. 154) and, most forcefully, Mattock (1989, p. 74f)".
    - All three quotations are verbatim in the Warwick copies (2011, p. 259; 2010, notes 11 and 7; the 2010 copy is a preprint with its own pagination). Neither paper mentions the Bayt al-Ḥikma. Gutas 1998, p. 140, is cited in 2010 n. 15 and 2011 nn. 53 and 57.

**Contested, flag in the post or caption**

24. **Life dates.**
    - 808 (EI2; his age in the letter) or 194/809–810 (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa).
    - Death in 873 (*Fihrist*, Ibn al-Qifṭī, EI2) or 6 Ṣafar 264, 18 November 877 (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa).
    - Captions say "about 808–873".
25. **Where he learned Greek.** Alexandria (Ibn Juljul, in Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29.9) or Byzantine lands (Ibn al-Qifṭī; DSB). Say only that the sources disagree.
26. **Basra and al-Khalīl.** Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa says Ḥunayn studied grammar with al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad (8.29.1, 8.29.8), and Ibn Juljul puts the meeting in Persia (8.29.9). DSB and EI2 say, after Plessner 1956, p. 244, that this is chronologically impossible. Do not state it.
27. **The autobiographical epistle.** Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa quotes it at length (8.29.14–15), but Rosenthal (1937) and Cooperson (1996) question its authenticity. Do not quote it as Ḥunayn's own words.
28. **The imprisonment under al-Mutawakkil.** The reports differ:
    - the poison test, from ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Jibrīl;
    - the icon episode, from Ibn Juljul;
    - Bukhtīshūʿ's slander, from Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa.
    - The length differs as well: EI2 says six months, and ʿUbayd Allāh's story says a year. Keep it out of the film.
29. **The Bayt al-Ḥikma.** Its nature is disputed:
    - Eche 1967: central to translation.
    - Balty-Guesdon 1992: cautious.
    - Gutas 1998: a minimal library, a bureau of an administration on the Sasanian model, with no part in translation from Greek.
    - Di Branco 2012: rejects the Sasanian origin, calls it a palace library on general Umayyad and Byzantine lines, and finds links to Greek translation through its staff.
    - İhsanoğlu 2023: the modern myth comes from European scholarship and mistranslated Arabic sources.
    - The film says "argues" and "a library in the caliphs' administration". It shows no building and no "academy".
30. **Jundishapur.** The "academy of Jundishapur" is another disputed institution. The brief did not research it, so the film names the place only.
31. **"Sense for sense".** That description comes from al-Ṣafadī in the fourteenth century and is schematic. The film's own claim rests on Ḥunayn's letter: he collated copies and judged translations by whether they conveyed the meaning (Vagelpohl 2010; Gutas 1998, p. 140, via Vagelpohl). The film does not say "sense for sense".
32. **Retina.** That medieval Latin retina in general comes from this book is not established. It is documented in the Latin version of Ḥunayn's book on the eye, in the Codex Gigas (f. 249ra) and in the 1515 print, and in the Latin *Isagoge*, in the Codex Gigas (f. 240ra) and in the 1483 *Articella* (fol. 2v). Other Arabic-Latin translations, such as Gerard of Cremona's Avicenna, may have spread it; Etymonline and Wiktionary say Gerard "may have" coined it. The film says only that the word "is already in" a Latin version of an Arabic book, which the Codex Gigas shows.
33. **Constantine's dates and the Latin *Isagoge*.**
    - Constantine died about 1087 (Meyerhof p. xxxiv; Library of Congress heading c. 1020–1087) or before 1098/99 (newer scholarship, Newton in Burnett and Jacquart 1994 and Kwakkel and Newton 2019, not read). The film gives no date for him, and the post says "late eleventh century" only.
    - The translator of the Latin *Isagoge* is usually given as Constantine or his circle. Jacquart 1986 (Persée summary) does not exclude Constantine but would date it to the start of his career; the Bodleian catalogue says Constantine. Call it the Latin version of the *Isagoge* and leave its translator unnamed.
33a. **The note on BL Arundel Or 10, f. 28r.** "انتحله حنين ومات من غير تتمة وانتحل حبيش تتمته" uses انتحل, "to claim another's work as one's own". A fuller form of the same note in another British Library copy (no. 6690, copied 829 AH), quoted by the 1978 editors (p. 25), calls the book "كتاب ايساغوجي لجالينوس" and says Ḥubaysh "ادعاه". It is a late copyist's charge that the *Masāʾil* was a work of Galen's taken over by Ḥunayn and Ḥubaysh. Do not render it as "Ḥunayn composed it" and do not use it as evidence of the method.
33b. **Authorship of the *Ten Treatises*.** Ḥunayn's own account (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa 8.29 list no. 2) and Meyerhof make the book his, but Meyerhof prints it as "ascribed to" him because Bergsträsser judged the surviving Arabic to be his pupils' redaction (pp. xlix–l). The film can say "His book on the eye" and "by Ḥunayn"; the post states the hedge.

38. **English paths.** Checked in checking pass 1 in Merriam-Webster, Etymonline and two OED summaries (the full OED and the MED were not opened). Retina, uvea and cornea came from medieval Latin. For conjunctiva the dictionaries say New Latin (1540s), although coniunctiva is in the medieval translations, so the post now says only that. 39. **Buḥrān.** Resolved in checking pass 1. The Syriac būḥrānā means "trial" and "crisis of an illness" (Payne Smith 1903, p. 37; CAL; SEDRA), Pognon 1903, p. XV, says the medical term passed into Arabic, and al-Khwārizmī calls it Syriac. Wiktionary's Arabic entry says Aramaic בחרנא; the brief had misquoted it. The Persian sense "crisis" in general is in Dehkhoda, and Steingass (1892) gives only the medical sense. The Turkish sense and its Arabic source are confirmed by the Türk Dil Kurumu dictionary, which also has kriz from French.
