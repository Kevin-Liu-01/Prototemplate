## 2. The post (single long-form post, no reply chain)

32.05° N, 34.75° E · Jaffa · 1881

In the autumn of 1881, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda (אליעזר בן־יהודה) and his wife Devora (דבורה) landed at Jaffa. He had resolved to speak only Hebrew, at home and with anyone who understood it. Jews had read, written and prayed in Hebrew without a break, but from about the second century to the nineteenth it was no one's mother tongue, and many of its names for modern things were phrases. A newspaper was a מכתב עתי, a "periodical letter," and eyeglasses were בתי עיניים, "houses for the eyes."

He had argued the case in an essay dated in Paris on 13 Adar 5639 (8 March 1879) and printed in Peretz Smolenskin's journal *HaShachar* in Vienna. The essay is mostly about land: the nation needed a center in the Land of Israel. In one sentence he adds that Hebrew could already be written about anything, "וגם לדבר בה יש לאל ידנו אם אך נחפוץ" (and to speak it, too, is in our power, if only we wish). His son later annotated that sentence as the first hint of the demand that Hebrew be spoken again.

On 1 January 1880, in a column titled "שתי מלות חדשות" (two new words) in *Magid Mishneh*, a Hebrew weekly published in Lyck, East Prussia, he rejected ספר מלים, "book of words," as a copy of German *Wörterbuch*: "על ידי התחבולה הזאת נהיתה שפתנו כמעט לשפת אשכנזית במלות עבריות" (by this device our language has become almost German in Hebrew words). Hebrew grammar, he argued, already had the means. A prefixed mem can mark an instrument, as in מַפְתֵּחַ (key) from פתח (to open). A final ־וֹן can mark "the thing that holds within it" a concept. From מִלָּה (word) he made מִלּוֹן (*milon*), and from the root ק־ל־ע (to sling) he made מַקְלֵעַ (*makle'a*) for a cannon. Today מקלע means a machine gun. The Academy of the Hebrew Language calls מלון the first word he coined, although his son dated it to a notebook of 1879.

In Jerusalem the family kept the rule. Their son Ben-Zion, later Itamar Ben-Avi (איתמר בן־אב״י), was born in the Old City on 15 Av 5642 (31 July 1882) by his father's account; some sources give 1885. Devora kept no servant, so that the child would hear no language but Hebrew, and his father called him "הילד העברי הראשון" (the first Hebrew child). That account comes from a memoir published in 1917–18, some thirty-five years later. Ghil'ad Zuckermann calls Ben-Avi "symbolically" the first native speaker and, citing Chaim Rabin, places the first community of speakers in the early twentieth century, among the children of graduates of the first Hebrew schools. Shlomo Izre'el ties the spread of spoken Hebrew to the Second Aliyah, a wave of immigrants in that century's first decade, and he calls the picture of Ben-Yehuda as the only reviver of the language "a myth." Ben-Yehuda supplied a program, many words and a dictionary, and the first communities of speakers formed in the schools and the farming settlements.

Many of the new words followed the method of the 1880 column. A Hebrew noun is a root of consonants (שורש) set into a pattern (משקל) of vowels and affixes, and some patterns mark a class of thing. The Academy calls מַקְטֵל, the pattern of מַפְתֵּחַ, "משקל המכשירים" (the pattern of instruments), and gives מַצְפֵּן (compass), מַגְהֵץ (iron) and מַחְשֵׁב (computer) as examples. The related pattern מַקְטֶלֶת gave מַגֶּבֶת (towel), from נ־ג־ב (to dry), which Ben-Yehuda marked as his own, and מַצֶּקֶת (ladle), from י־צ־ק (to pour), which the language committee coined in 1912–13. The dual ending ־ַיִם, used for pairs, turned אוֹפָן (wheel) into the bicycle. The dictionary prints that word אָפְנַיִם and names its model in a footnote, אָזְנַיִם, ears; today it is pointed אוֹפַנַּיִם.

The dictionary records this work on its own pages. The first volume of the *Complete Dictionary of Ancient and Modern Hebrew* (מלון הלשון העברית הישנה והחדשה) began to appear in fascicles in 1908, published in Berlin-Schöneberg by Langenscheidt. Its title page promises "מספר רב של מלים אשר יצר המחבר למושגים ישנים וחדשים" (a large number of words that the author created for old and new concepts). Its key of signs marks the period of each word, from the Bible to the literature after the Talmud, and a small looped sign marks "מלים שחדשתי אני ושנתקבלו כבר בספרות של זמננו, או בדבור העברי בארץ ישראל" (words that I coined and that have already been accepted in the literature of our time, or in Hebrew speech in the Land of Israel). It stands before the bicycle, a word that "has already been used much in the newspapers and in books," and, in the second volume, before גְּלִידָה, ice cream.

Other modern words are old ones with new senses. Ezekiel's first vision reads "וּמִתּוֹכָהּ כְּעֵין הַחַשְׁמַל מִתּוֹךְ הָאֵשׁ" (and from its midst, like the look of the *khashmal*, from the midst of the fire). The meaning of חשמל is uncertain, and the Septuagint rendered it ἤλεκτρον. The poet Judah Leib Gordon (יהודה לייב גורדון) followed the Greek and used the word for electricity. His note, which the dictionary quotes, explains: "כונתי להכח הטבעי הנקרא עלעקטריציטט שכן תרגום היוני של חשמל הוא עלעקטראן" (I mean the natural force called *Elektrizität*, since the Greek translation of *khashmal* is *elektron*). Ben-Yehuda did not mark the word as his. He called the Greek reading a homiletic gloss and accepted the usage because need had made it accepted in speech and in literature. In 1893 Yehiel Michel Pines (יחיאל מיכל פינס) ranked such words highest: "הגדולה שבמעלות למלה חדשה אם איננה חדשה" (the highest rank for a new word is if it is not new).

Several of the new words have more than one claimant. Pines put שָׁעוֹן (clock) into print in 1885, and Ben-Yehuda credited it to him in 1898, although the scholar Raphael Weiss later argued that Pines had only endorsed it. In December 1892, after the railway from Jaffa to Jerusalem opened, David Yellin (דוד ילין) proposed קַטָּר for the locomotive, fitting Arabic *qiṭār* to a Hebrew pattern and to קִיטוֹר (steam). Ben-Yehuda answered with רַכֶּבֶת (train) and credited Pines with its pattern, that of the Talmud's חַמֶּרֶת and גַּמֶּלֶת, caravans of donkeys and of camels. Reuven Sivan concludes that Pines "apparently" coined the word for tomato, עַגְבָנִיָּה, in 5646 (1885–86), from the root ע־ג־ב (to desire), after German *Liebesapfel*, "love apple." Ben-Yehuda found it immodest, kept it out of his home, his papers and his dictionary, and used בַּדּוּרָה, from Arabic *bandūra*. The dictionary's sign does not settle these questions: a volume printed long after Ben-Yehuda's death marks שעון as his.

The words reached speakers through newspapers, schools and committees. Ben-Yehuda's newspaper, *HaZvi* (הצבי), first appeared on 24 October 1884 and ran, as *HaZvi* and as *HaOr*, until 1915. A society formed in Jerusalem in 1889 set up a committee to collect Hebrew words, and both soon lapsed. Because the teachers' association needed terms for its classes, a new language committee was founded in Jerusalem in 1904–05, with Ben-Yehuda, Yellin and Pines among its members. Its first bulletin, in 1912, printed a list of arithmetic terms.

The dictionary's first volume names the Hilfsverein der Deutschen Juden (the Aid Society of German Jews) among its funders and the society's founder, Paul Nathan, on its publication committee. The Hilfsverein was building a technical school in Haifa, the Technikum, and on 26 October 1913 the school's board resolved, in the Hebrew rendering the Academy prints, that "מדעי הטבע יהיו נלמדים בשפה הגרמנית" (the natural sciences will be taught in the German language). Three board members, Ahad Ha'am, Shmaryahu Levin and Yehiel Tschlenow, resigned, and on 18 November a public meeting in Jaffa took up Hebrew as the language of instruction in the Hebrew schools. By 1914 the school was to teach in Hebrew, and the Technion opened in 1924 teaching in Hebrew.

Article 22 of the Mandate for Palestine, done at London on 24 July 1922, reads: "English, Arabic and Hebrew shall be the official languages of Palestine." Ben-Yehuda died in Jerusalem that December, with five volumes of the dictionary in print. Four more appeared between 1925 and 1939, and N. H. Tur-Sinai completed it in 1959. On 27 August 1953 the Knesset created the Academy of the Hebrew Language, gave it the task "לכוון את התפתחות הלשון העברית על יסוד חקר הלשון לתקופותיה ולענפיה" (to direct the development of the Hebrew language on the basis of research into the language in its periods and branches), and dissolved the committee into it.

The usual name for this history is "revival." Izre'el argues that Hebrew had never died, and he quotes the linguist Haim Blanc: its unusual feature was that it "was no one's mother tongue." He calls "(re)vernacularization" probably the best term for the social change, and he uses "emergence" for the formation of the spoken language. Zuckermann argues that the language that emerged is a hybrid, with Hebrew and Yiddish as its primary contributors, and he calls it "Israeli." By his Founder Principle, the Yiddish of the first settlers shaped it.

In the Chinese series of these posts, Matteo Ricci and Xu Guangqi gave Euclid a Chinese vocabulary in 1607 by joining a common character to one qualifier, so that 直線 read as "straight line" on first sight. The Hebrew coiners had a more regular device: a root the reader already knew, set into a pattern that marked the class of thing. Both projects also took old words and changed their senses. 幾何, "how much," became geometry, and Ezekiel's חשמל became electricity.

If you have worked with Ben-Yehuda's papers or the posthumous volumes of the dictionary: who decided which entries in those volumes received his coinage sign, given that it stands on שעון, a word he had credited to Pines in 1898?

## 5. Fact-check list

**Verified against a primary or scholarly source**
1. The 1879 essay: dated "פאריז, י״ג אדר" (8 March 1879), printed in *HaShachar* year 9, no. 7, Nisan 5639, signed "בן־יהודה"; the quoted sentences on land and on speech; Ben-Avi's note calling the speech sentence "the first hint." Sivan 1978 edition via Project Ben-Yehuda (read/257).
2. "שתי מלות חדשות," *Magid Mishneh*, 17 Tevet 5640 (1 January 1880): the attack on ספר מלים as a copy of *Wörterbuch*; the rule for prefixed mem and final ־וֹן; מלון from מלה; מקלע for "cannon" on the model of מפתח and מסרק, against another writer's compound קנה און. Scan; Academy page (2018), which calls מלון his first coinage. The column points the word מַקְלֵעַ (checked on the scan). Modern sense "machine gun": Wiktionary.
3. Arrival at Jaffa before Sukkot 5642 (autumn 1881) and the carriage to Jerusalem through the Jaffa Gate; the line "אני מדבר עברית, ורק עברית" (Eliezer about himself); Devora keeping no servant so the child would hear only Hebrew; the son's birth on 15 Av 5642 by Ben-Yehuda's account; the phrase "ונולד הילד העברי הראשון" in Ben-Yehuda's own text (read/5692, read/4345). The memoir appeared in *HaToren* in 1917–18 (Ne'eman 1973/74; Project Ben-Yehuda's notes read "כך ב'התורן'").
4. Dictionary vol. 1: title page "ירושלם," "ברלין," "אתת״מ לחרבן" (year 1840) and the promise of words "אשר יצר המחבר" (leaf n12); Mirkin's conversion to 5669 (August 1908 to July 1909) and the first fascicle in early 1908 (Mirkin, p. 313); Gilboa's era count for *HaZvi* issue 1 (p. 308) confirms the offset. **Contested, flag in the post or caption**
16. "First Hebrew child" and "first native speaker": Ben-Yehuda's own phrase, printed in 1917–18, about thirty-five years after the birth. Ben-Avi's birth year: 1882 (memoir; he.wikipedia citing Karni 1973 and Lang 2008) against 1885 (first edition of his autobiography, per he.wikipedia; Gilboa lists him as 1885–1943). Zuckermann also calls Dola Ben-Yehuda Wittmann "one of the first native Israeli-speakers."
17. Ben-Yehuda's individual role against the schools and settlements; "revival" against "(re)vernacularization" or "emergence"; Zuckermann's hybrid thesis. No published critique of the hybrid thesis was opened; if the post names critics, check first.
18. Credit for particular words: רכבת (Pines's pattern, Ben-Yehuda's word; Sivan adds Yellin's inspiration; at its own entry the dictionary gives it the sign for words coined by others); שעון (Pines per the Academy 2019 and Ben-Yehuda's 1898 note, against Weiss, p. 239 n. 45, who argues Pines only endorsed it); אווירון (Ben-Avi or Ben-Yehuda); מלון as the first coinage (the Academy and Weiss say 1880; Ben-Avi points to an 1879 notebook and names רודה and בעלי השתפות in the 1879 essay as earlier creations); the coinage sign in posthumous volumes.
19. The committee's founding and first list: 1890 with the name ועד הלשון (he.wikipedia) against late 1889 as Safa Berura's committee and 5665 (1904/05) as the founding (Academy). *100 מילים* calls the 1912 arithmetic list "רשימת המילים הראשונה," while the Academy's committee page says several lists had already appeared in Ben-Yehuda's papers before 1912. The post says "formed in 1889" and "founded in 1904–05," and calls the 1912 list the first bulletin's list.
20. Conflicts inside the sources. Ben-Yehuda's letter to Bacher calls the sign "סימן יד או כוכב" (a hand or a star), while vol. 1 prints a looped mark. The memoir dates the *HaHavatzelet* issue with his first article "ערב שבת י״ב תשרי תרמ״ב," but 12 Tishrei 5642 was a Wednesday; the memoir gives no day of arrival, only "before Sukkot." The Jaffa poster prints no year: Commons, from the NLI collection, gives 1913, but Tuesday 18 Heshvan also fell in 1912, 1915 and 1916.
