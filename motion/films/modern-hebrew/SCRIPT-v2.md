# modern-hebrew: the script, version 2

This file replaces the words of `SCRIPT.md` for the next build. `SCRIPT.md` stays as the record of the 100 s cut. The look, type, palette and set pieces are still `CONCEPT.md`'s. Only the changes listed below under Visual changes are new.

Kevin's notes on the 100 s cut were that it was slow, the video was not interesting and the script was weird, and that the diagrams and visuals were good. This version keeps the set pieces and rewrites what is said over them. The film now says what was at stake, explains each word as it is built, and ends on a reversal.

## The story in one sentence

For about seventeen centuries Hebrew was no one's mother tongue, so Eliezer Ben-Yehuda built new words from its own roots and marked his accepted words in his dictionary with a small sign, and the tomato word he refused to print is the word Hebrew speakers use today.

## The decision

Three rewrites were scored out of 50, on five criteria of 10 each.

| script | interesting | clear | pace | facts | rules | total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The problem (seventeen centuries, phrases, the fixes, the refused word) | 8 | 8 | 8 | 9 | 8 | **41** |
| The reveal (opens on the refused tomato word) | 8 | 6 | 8 | 7 | 7 | 36 |
| The word (follows milon and agvaniya) | 6 | 6 | 5 | 8 | 7 | 32 |

The problem won. Its shape is the one the stranger needs: the stakes first, then the method in three builds, then the sign, then a turn and a payoff. These changes were made to it:
- The Ezekiel beat is now two lines, taken from The reveal. The first says no one is sure what khashmal meant, and the second says why a poet used it for electricity.
- One new line says Ben-Yehuda did not mark the electricity word as his own, over p. 1806, where the sign comes down and withdraws. The diagnosis asked for this. The sign now does something different at each word. It lands on the bicycle, it withdraws from electricity, and it finds no place at the tomato.
- The title page opens the film and comes back under "In his own dictionary", taken from The word. The viewer sees מִלּוֹן isolated at the open, watches it built at 20 s, and reads it again as the first word of the book at 32 s.
- The milon line is shorter and has no clause stacked on clause.
- The eyeglasses example moves from the narration to a gloss on card 1, which saves 5 s.
- The native reader's one-word ending is cut, because a one-word utterance breaks the rules. The film ends on the narrator's payoff line and then the wordless sign going home.
- Every gloss and note on screen is now a complete sentence, as in The problem. The English running heads and the card labels are removed.

What the film no longer tells is the story of 1913 and 1922: the funders' page, the board (the Technikum, the Jaffa poster and Article 22) and the close. Told with its reasons, the Technikum dispute needs about 17 s and three more names, and the film cannot hold it under 75 s together with the word-making story. MOTION.md's roster entry says that 1913 and 1922 close the story, so this departs from the roster on purpose. Kevin decides whether it stands. The scans and their notes stay in the composition's assets for a longer cut.

## Length and pace

- **Length:** about 72 s (it was 100 s). Speech is about 64 s of that. The rest is 13 gaps of 0.35 s, a 1.0 s hold after the composing card's drop, a 0.8 s hold after the bicycle build, a 0.3 s lead and a 1.6 s wordless close.
- **Words:** 183 written in 14 narrator lines, and 184 spoken, because 1880 is read "eighteen eighty". The estimate uses Clara's measured pace in `sound/manifest.json`: about 3.1 words a second on plain lines and 2.6 on lines with names or Hebrew words.
- **Pace:** the longest stretch without speech is the 1.6 s close. No picture holds longer than its line needs, except where a reading floor sets the hold.
- **Times** in the table below are estimates for the build. Place every take by its `.json` timings, never change a take's speed, and let the holds absorb the difference.
